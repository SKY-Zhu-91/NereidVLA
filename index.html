THE RESEARCH QUESTION

Can marine spatial knowledge
be transferred to navigation?

Underwater navigation requires translating visual spatial cues into motion, yet marine vision-language datasets rarely include paired robot trajectories. NereidVLA connects these two forms of learning: first understand the underwater world, then learn how to move through it.

We train Sea2Know on approximately two million marine examples to learn target grounding, spatial relations, and scene structure without paired robot-motion supervision. We then adapt it with expert trajectories executed under simulated underwater vehicle dynamics. Given RGB histories and a language instruction, NereidVLA predicts discrete motion tokens that decode into local 3D trajectories for closed-loop tracking.

Evaluation spans an independent six-dimensional understanding benchmark, offline trajectory prediction, simulated navigation, and physical execution in a laboratory pool. Under matched downstream settings, marine spatial initialization improves simulation success from 72.4% to 84.4%.

TWO-STAGE FRAMEWORK

Understand the scene. Learn the motion.

Marine observations establish spatial knowledge; dynamics-grounded demonstrations connect that knowledge to action.

STAGE 1 · SEA2KNOW

Marine spatial learning

Full-parameter vision-language fine-tuning of Qwen3-VL-Instruct learns grounding, spatial relations, depth ordering, scene structure, and temporal changes. Training combines existing marine annotations and question formats with underwater simulation scenes.

Approximately 2M examples · No paired robot trajectories

STAGE 2 · NEREIDVLA

Trajectory-supervised adaptation

Sea2Know (8B) initializes the navigation policy. Expert motion recorded under simulated vehicle dynamics supervises motion-token prediction. The visual encoder is frozen; the language model, embeddings, and LM head are updated without LoRA.

100,000 training samples · Executed-motion supervision

METHOD

From visual history to local 3D motion

A vision-language policy predicts waypoints; a feedback controller turns them into executed motion.

  1. Observe and interpret

    Four front-facing RGB frames at t − 1.5, t − 1.0, t − 0.5, and t seconds are paired with the language instruction.

  2. Predict motion tokens

    The model predicts ten motion tokens and EOS. A fixed codebook contains 1,024 3D displacement entries; residual-aware encoding lets later tokens compensate for earlier quantization errors.

  3. Decode the trajectory

    Cumulative displacements form ten waypoints at 0.5-second intervals: a five-second horizon in the forward–left–up body frame fixed at decision time.

  4. Track and replan

    A low-level controller uses PID feedback and dynamics feedforward to track the trajectory. New visual observations support closed-loop replanning.

Policy input is RGB + language. Robot state estimates support trajectory supervision and low-level control; they are not additional inputs to the VLA.
Demonstrations and training details

MarineGym demonstration collection spans 573 scenes and 5,117 expert demonstrations across seabeds, fixed facilities, pipelines, aquaculture cages, and caves. Training uses 100,000 samples from 4,597 demonstrations in 516 scenes, with 18,726 additional validation samples. Offline evaluation uses approximately 10,000 samples; offline and closed-loop tests use 50 independent held-out scenes with unseen assets, layouts, and structures.

Stage 2 runs for one epoch (391 optimizer steps) on 32 A800 80GB GPUs, with global batch size 256 and learning rate 10⁻⁵. Cross-entropy is applied to motion tokens and EOS; context tokens are masked. Targets are executed robot trajectories, rather than geometric reference paths.

How closed-loop success, efficiency, and smoothness are measured

The policy replans at 1 Hz of simulated time. Success requires reaching the task goal within the time limit and without safety violations; collisions and timeouts are failures. Success is measured from executed motion.

Grid-SPL weights success by path efficiency relative to robot-aware A* paths on a 0.25-m 3D occupancy grid. Higher is better. Jerk RMS measures translational smoothness in m/s³; lower is better. It averages all runs, including failures, using simulated time.
